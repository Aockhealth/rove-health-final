## **How PMOS Is Actually Diagnosed — Not Just "Irregular Periods and a Scan"**

### ***The Rotterdam Criteria, Explained Without the Jargon***

### **Introduction: The Diagnosis That Gets Handed Out Too Fast, and Too Slow**

Two very different things happen to Indian women and PMOS. Some are told they have it after a single ultrasound shows a few follicles — no blood test, no real conversation. Others spend years bounced between doctors, told their reports are "borderline," never told clearly whether they actually meet the criteria or not.

Both are the same failure: nobody walked them through what the diagnosis actually requires.

There is a real, internationally agreed definition. It isn't a vibe, and it isn't one ultrasound. Knowing it means you can ask your doctor a sharper question than "do I have PMOS?" — you can ask "which two of the three do I meet, and what did we rule out?"

## **What This Article Will Help You Understand**

* The three official criteria and why you only need two of them
* What has to be ruled out first — because PMOS is a diagnosis of exclusion
* Why a scan alone is never enough, and why a normal scan doesn't clear you either

## **The Three Criteria (You Need Two of Three)**

This is the Rotterdam criteria — the framework most gynaecologists worldwide, including in India, are trained on:

**1. Irregular or absent ovulation**
Cycles longer than 35 days, fewer than 8–9 periods a year, or cycles that come but without a real ovulation behind them. This is the one Rove's own tracking can actually help document — a pattern of anovulatory cycles logged over months is real evidence, not a guess.

**2. Signs of excess androgens — clinical or on a blood test**
Clinically: hirsutism (coarse hair growth on the face, chest, or lower abdomen), persistent jawline acne, or scalp hair thinning. On a blood test: elevated free or total testosterone. You don't need both — either counts.

**3. Polycystic-appearing ovaries on ultrasound**
Multiple small follicles (usually defined as 20 or more per ovary on modern high-resolution ultrasound, or an increased ovarian volume) — but this criterion alone means very little. A large number of women without PMOS have ovaries that look like this, especially in their early twenties.

**The rule: two of these three, not just one.** A polycystic-looking scan with regular ovulation and no androgen signs is not PMOS. Irregular cycles with a normal scan and no androgen signs can still be PMOS, if a blood test shows elevated androgens.

## **What Has To Be Ruled Out First**

This is the part that gets skipped most often. PMOS is what doctors call a diagnosis of exclusion — before landing on it, a few other conditions that can look identical need to be checked and ruled out:

* **Thyroid dysfunction** (TSH) — an underactive thyroid alone can cause irregular cycles and weight changes that mimic PMOS
* **High prolactin** (a simple blood test) — can stop ovulation entirely, unrelated to PMOS
* **Non-classic congenital adrenal hyperplasia** — a rarer condition that can produce the same androgen symptoms
* **Cushing's syndrome** — in specific clinical scenarios, when there are other suggestive signs

If these weren't checked, the diagnosis isn't complete yet — regardless of what the ultrasound showed.

## **Why This Matters for What Happens Next**

The specific combination you meet changes the actual treatment conversation:

* Irregular ovulation + androgen signs, normal-looking ovaries → the metabolic/insulin-resistance pathway is usually the bigger lever
* Polycystic ovaries + irregular cycles, no androgen signs at all → a different, often milder presentation
* All three → the picture doctors most associate with the classic phenotype, usually with more pronounced insulin resistance

This is also why "I was told I have PMOS" is worth revisiting if you were never told which criteria you met, or whether thyroid and prolactin were actually checked.

## **What to Bring to That Appointment**

* A record of your actual cycle lengths over the last 6+ months — not a memory of "they're irregular"
* Ask specifically: TSH, prolactin, and either free or total testosterone
* Ask which two (or three) criteria your case meets, in those words

## **Key Takeaways**

* PMOS needs 2 of 3: irregular ovulation, androgen signs, polycystic ovaries on scan
* A scan alone is never a diagnosis — plenty of normal ovaries look polycystic
* Thyroid and prolactin problems must be ruled out first — they can mimic PMOS exactly
* Ask which criteria you actually met, not just "do I have it"

## **Dr. Rove's Note**

If you were diagnosed from an ultrasound alone, with no TSH, prolactin, or androgen testing on record, it's worth a follow-up conversation — not because the diagnosis is necessarily wrong, but because you deserve to know it was actually confirmed, not assumed.
