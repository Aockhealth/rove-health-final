## **Starting Letrozole or Clomid? Here's What Actually Changes About Your Cycle**

### ***A Guide to Ovulation-Induction Medication — What to Expect, Not What to Take***

### **Introduction: The Part Nobody Explains Before You Start**

If your doctor has mentioned Letrozole or Clomiphene (Clomid), it's usually because your cycles aren't ovulating reliably on their own — often, though not always, connected to PMOS. What almost never gets explained clearly is what these medications actually do to the shape of your cycle, and why tracking through a medicated cycle looks different from tracking a natural one.

This article won't tell you what dose to take — that's between you and your doctor, and it depends on things specific to you. What it will do is explain what to expect, so a medicated cycle doesn't look alarming when it's actually working exactly as intended.

## **What This Article Will Help You Understand**

* What these medications are actually doing, in plain terms
* Why your usual signals (temperature, test strips) still matter — and why the timing shifts
* What's a normal side effect, and what's worth calling your doctor about

## **What These Medications Actually Do**

Both Letrozole and Clomiphene work on the same basic idea, through different mechanisms: they trick your brain into producing more of the hormone (FSH) that recruits and matures a follicle, because your body senses less estrogen feedback than it actually has. The goal is one dominant, mature follicle that goes on to ovulate — not "more eggs," despite how that's sometimes described casually.

Letrozole is now the first-line choice in most current guidelines, particularly for PMOS-related ovulatory dysfunction — evidence over the last decade has generally favored it over Clomiphene, especially in women with PMOS specifically. Clomiphene is still used, and both are taken for a short window early in the cycle, on days your doctor specifies.

## **How the Cycle Actually Changes**

* **Ovulation is usually later, not earlier**, than an unmedicated cycle for the same woman — often several days later than day 14. This is completely expected and not a sign the medication isn't working.
* **Your test strips still matter.** The LH surge still happens and is still the signal to watch for — the medication changes *when* it happens, not whether tracking it still works.
* **Your temperature shift still confirms it.** A thermal shift after ovulation is just as real and just as useful on a medicated cycle.
* **Some cycles won't produce a mature follicle at all**, especially the first cycle on a new dose — this is why your doctor may want a monitoring ultrasound rather than relying on tracking alone, particularly early on.

## **Logging a Medicated Cycle**

If you're tracking in Rove, log the medication and the day you took it the same way you'd log anything else — this matters because it tells the app (and eventually your doctor, if you export your report) that a later-than-usual ovulation this cycle is expected, not a new problem showing up.

## **What's a Normal Side Effect vs. What to Call About**

Commonly expected, usually manageable: hot flashes, mood changes, mild bloating, headache — typically limited to the days around when you take it.

Worth calling your doctor about: pelvic pain that's severe or one-sided, visual disturbances, or significant bloating that doesn't settle — these are uncommon but are the reason monitoring exists.

## **The Question Worth Asking Your Doctor**

"Are we tracking this with monitoring (bloodwork or ultrasound), or with home signals, or both?" The answer changes how much weight to put on a strip or a temperature chart in any given cycle.

## **Key Takeaways**

* Letrozole and Clomid don't replace your usual tracking — they shift its timing, usually later
* A delayed LH surge or thermal shift on a medicated cycle is expected, not a red flag
* Log the medication itself — it changes how a later ovulation should be read
* Severe one-sided pain or visual changes are the actual reasons to call your doctor, not routine hot flashes

## **Dr. Rove's Note**

The most common reason women stop trusting their tracking mid-treatment is an ovulation that "came late" — on a medicated cycle, that's usually the medication working as intended, not the tracking failing. Ask your doctor to walk you through what a normal medicated cycle should look like for your specific protocol before you start.
