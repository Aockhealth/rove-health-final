## **"But My Friend's PMOS Looks Nothing Like Mine" — That's Because It Isn't One Condition**

### ***The Four PMOS Phenotypes, and Why the Same Advice Doesn't Work for All of Them***

### **Introduction: Same Name, Different Bodies**

One woman with PMOS is thin, has regular-ish cycles, but can't get rid of jawline acne and dark facial hair. Another is heavier, has cycles that vanish for months, and no androgen symptoms at all. Both were handed the same pamphlet: "lose weight, cut sugar."

They don't have the same PMOS. Clinically, they may not even be in the same phenotype.

Since the Rotterdam criteria only require 2 of 3 features (see "How PMOS Is Actually Diagnosed"), there are several genuinely different combinations that all get called "PMOS" — and they don't all respond to the same first move.

## **What This Article Will Help You Understand**

* The four recognized PMOS phenotypes and which criteria each one meets
* Why "Phenotype A" tends to carry the most metabolic risk, and why that matters
* Why your phenotype should change what you try first — not just what you eat less of

## **The Four Phenotypes**

**Phenotype A — Full house**
Irregular ovulation + androgen excess + polycystic ovaries on scan. All three. This is the presentation most associated with insulin resistance and the metabolic picture — usually the one where inositol, metformin, and the insulin-focused approach (see "Inositol or Metformin for PMOS") have the strongest evidence behind them.

**Phenotype B — Ovulatory dysfunction + androgen excess, normal-looking ovaries**
Irregular cycles and androgen signs (acne, hirsutism, or elevated blood androgens), but the ultrasound doesn't show the polycystic pattern. Still carries real metabolic risk — the ovary appearance isn't what's driving it.

**Phenotype C — "Ovulatory PMOS"**
Regular-ish ovulation, but androgen excess plus polycystic ovaries on scan. Cycles may look normal enough that this phenotype gets missed entirely — she's not the one complaining about irregular periods, she's the one with acne and unwanted hair growth that nobody's connected to her ovaries.

**Phenotype D — Non-hyperandrogenic**
Irregular ovulation + polycystic ovaries, but no clinical or blood-test androgen excess at all. Generally the mildest metabolic picture of the four, and the one most likely to be over-diagnosed from a scan alone.

## **Why This Actually Changes What You Should Try First**

* **Phenotype A and B** — insulin resistance is usually central. This is where the muscle-and-protein approach in "The Indian Lean PMOS," and the inositol-vs-metformin conversation, matter most directly.
* **Phenotype C** — the ovulation isn't the problem; the androgen excess is. Skin and hair symptoms (see "Acne & Hormones," "The Dark Neck") deserve equal attention to cycle tracking, not less.
* **Phenotype D** — often the phenotype where lifestyle change alone, without medication, gets furthest — but also the one worth double-checking wasn't over-called from an ultrasound alone.

None of this replaces your doctor working out your actual phenotype from your own results — this is here so the conversation makes sense when you have it.

## **The Indian Context**

Phenotype A and B — the ones with the strongest insulin-resistance link — show up disproportionately in South Asian women, partly because of the "thin-fat" metabolic pattern covered in "The Indian Lean PMOS." A normal BMI does not mean you're Phenotype D.

## **Key Takeaways**

* PMOS is 4 recognized phenotypes, not one condition with one severity dial
* Phenotype A/B carry the most metabolic risk and usually respond most to insulin-focused approaches
* Phenotype C is easy to miss because the cycles look fine
* Ask your doctor which combination of the three criteria you actually meet — that's effectively your phenotype

## **Dr. Rove's Note**

If your treatment plan is "lose weight" and nothing else, ask which phenotype that's based on — it's the right first move for some phenotypes and close to irrelevant for others.
